Readability Scores Explained: Flesch, Grade Level and More

What readability scores measure

Readability scores estimate how difficult a piece of writing is to understand, using measurable features such as sentence length and syllables per word. They do not judge whether the ideas are good or the facts are correct; they measure the surface difficulty of the language. A high score means most readers can follow the text easily, while a low score means the text demands more effort, education, or concentration.

The best-known measure is the Flesch Reading Ease score, which runs from 0 to 100. Scores of 90 to 100 are very easy, roughly primary-school level, while scores of 60 to 70 are standard, readable by most adults, and scores below 30 are very difficult, typical of academic and legal writing. The related Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level converts the same features into a US school grade, so a grade level of 8 means an average eighth-grade student should understand the text.

Why readability matters

Readable writing reaches more people. About half of adults read below the level of a typical newspaper, so text written at university level excludes a large share of potential readers. Clear writing is not dumbed-down writing; it is writing that respects the reader's time and attention. Government agencies, health services, and consumer companies increasingly test readability because misunderstood instructions have real consequences, from missed medication doses to unclaimed benefits.

For websites, readability affects engagement as well. Readers abandon dense pages quickly, and search engines notice when visitors leave fast. Shorter sentences, everyday words, and clear structure keep readers on the page and help them act on what they read. Aiming for a Flesch score of 60 to 70 suits most general audiences, while specialist audiences can tolerate lower scores when the topic demands technical terms.

How to improve readability

Shorten your sentences first, because sentence length is the biggest driver of most scores. Break any sentence over 25 words into two, and vary the rhythm so the text does not sound choppy. Replace long words with shorter ones where the meaning stays the same: use instead of utilise, help instead of facilitate. Remove jargon unless your audience genuinely shares it, and define any technical term the first time you use it.

Structure helps as much as word choice. One idea per paragraph, a clear subheading every few paragraphs, and lists for sequences all lower the effort of reading. Read the text aloud before publishing; if you stumble, the reader will too. Test your draft with a readability checker and aim for steady improvement rather than a perfect score, because some topics will always need longer words and sentences.

Putting It Into Practice

Take a recent piece of your own writing and run it through a readability checker, noting the score and grade level. Then rewrite the three longest sentences, replace five long words with shorter ones, and split any paragraph over six sentences. Check the score again and read the revised version aloud to confirm it still sounds like you. Repeat this exercise monthly, and plain language will become a habit rather than an editing chore, reaching more readers without losing any of your ideas.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a good Flesch Reading Ease score?

60 to 70 is standard and readable by most adults. 70 to 80 is fairly easy; below 50 is difficult for general readers.

What grade level should I write for?

Grade 8 is a common target for general audiences. Specialist content for experts can be higher.

Do readability scores measure quality?

No. They measure surface difficulty only. A readable text can still be wrong, and a difficult text can be excellent.
