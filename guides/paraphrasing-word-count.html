Paraphrasing and Word Count: Rewriting Without Changing Meaning

What paraphrasing does to word count

Paraphrasing usually changes the word count, sometimes by a lot. A dense sentence rewritten in plain language often becomes longer, because unpacking a complex idea takes more words, while a wordy passage rewritten tightly can become shorter. There is no rule that a paraphrase must match the original length; the goal is to preserve the meaning in your own words, not to match a number.

Students sometimes worry that a paraphrase with a different word count looks suspicious, but markers judge paraphrases on meaning and originality, not length. A paraphrase that keeps the original sentence structure with a few words swapped is poor paraphrasing regardless of length, while a thorough rewrite that changes structure and vocabulary is good paraphrasing even if it is twice as long. Focus on understanding the idea first, then explain it as you would to a friend.

How to paraphrase well

Read the passage until you understand it, then put it aside and write the idea from memory. This forces you to use your own structure rather than copying the original sentence shape. Compare your version with the original afterwards, checking that the meaning matches and that no distinctive phrases have survived unchanged. If a phrase is unique or technical, either quote it directly with citation or replace it with a genuinely different expression.

Keep the level of detail appropriate to your purpose. A paraphrase in an essay should preserve the claims that matter for your argument and may drop examples that do not. A paraphrase of a definition should preserve precision, because changing a technical term can change the meaning. When in doubt, quote the original and cite it rather than risking a distorted paraphrase.

Paraphrasing, word count and plagiarism

Paraphrasing does not remove the need to cite. Even when every word is your own, the idea belongs to its source, and failing to cite it is plagiarism. Citation rules differ by style guide, so follow the one your institution requires, and cite paraphrases just as you would quotations. A word counter can show you how much your version differs in length, but it cannot tell you whether the paraphrase is adequate; only a careful comparison with the source can do that.

Be especially careful with numbers, names, and technical terms, which should stay the same in a paraphrase. Changing a statistic or a proper name to make the text look more original is a serious error. Good paraphrasing is an act of understanding, and its value lies in showing that you have grasped the idea well enough to explain it yourself.

Putting It Into Practice

Practise paraphrasing this week: take a paragraph from a textbook, read it twice, close the book, and rewrite the idea in your own words. Compare your version with the original, checking that the meaning matches, the structure differs, and no distinctive phrase survives. Add the citation even though it is a practice exercise, so citing paraphrases becomes automatic. This single habit will improve your essays more than any paraphrasing tool, because it proves you understood the idea rather than rearranged its words.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does paraphrasing change the word count?

Yes, usually. Plain-language rewrites often get longer, while tight rewrites can get shorter. Length does not matter; meaning does.

Do I need to cite a paraphrase?

Yes. The idea still belongs to its source, so cite paraphrases just as you would direct quotations.

How do I know my paraphrase is good enough?

Compare it with the original: the meaning should match, the structure should differ, and no distinctive phrases should survive unchanged.
